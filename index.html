Q.1(a) Differentiate molecular dispersion and colloidal dispersion with example.[06] ▼

Molecular Dispersion vs Colloidal Dispersion

Molecular Dispersion (True Solution): A homogeneous system in which the solute particles are dispersed as individual molecules or ions throughout the solvent. The particle size is less than 1 nm.

Colloidal Dispersion: A heterogeneous system in which the dispersed phase particles have a size range of 1 nm to 1000 nm (0.001 μm to 1 μm). The particles are larger than molecules but too small to be seen by the naked eye.

Q.1(b) Describe any two kinetic properties of colloids.[05] ▼

Kinetic Properties of Colloids

Kinetic properties are those that arise due to the continuous random motion of colloidal particles resulting from their bombardment by molecules of the dispersion medium. The two important kinetic properties are:

1. Brownian Motion

Definition: Brownian motion is the continuous, random, zig-zag movement of colloidal particles suspended in a dispersion medium, first observed by Robert Brown (1827) in pollen grains.

Cause: It results from the unequal bombardment of colloidal particles by the molecules of the dispersion medium. Since colloidal particles are small enough, the impact of molecules from different directions does not cancel out completely, resulting in a net force that changes direction constantly.

Characteristics:

  • Smaller particles show more intense Brownian motion
  • It decreases with increase in viscosity of the medium
  • It decreases with increase in particle size
  • It is responsible for the stability of colloids (prevents settling)
  • The path traced by a particle is highly irregular

Einstein's Equation for Brownian Motion:

x̄ = √(2·D·t) = √(2·R·T·t / N·6πηr)

Where:

  • x̄ = Mean displacement in time t
  • D = Diffusion coefficient
  • R = Gas constant, T = Absolute temperature
  • N = Avogadro's number
  • η = Viscosity of medium, r = Radius of particle

Significance:

  • Confirms the molecular kinetic theory
  • Provides a method to determine Avogadro's number
  • Prevents sedimentation of colloidal particles (kinetic stability)
  • Forms the basis of Perrin's work (Nobel Prize 1926)

2. Diffusion

Definition: Diffusion is the spontaneous movement of colloidal particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, driven by the concentration gradient.

Fick's First Law of Diffusion:

dm/dt = -D · A · (dc/dx)

Where:

  • dm/dt = Amount of substance diffusing per unit time
  • D = Diffusion coefficient (cm²/s)
  • A = Cross-sectional area of diffusion
  • dc/dx = Concentration gradient

Einstein-Stokes Equation:

D = R·T / (N · 6πηr)

Where R = Gas constant, T = Temperature, N = Avogadro's number, η = Viscosity, r = Radius of particle.

Characteristics of Colloidal Diffusion:

  • Colloidal particles diffuse much more slowly than molecules in true solution
  • Diffusion coefficient is inversely proportional to particle size
  • It is influenced by temperature (increases with T) and viscosity (decreases with η)
  • Diffusion in colloids may deviate from ideal Fickian behavior

Significance:

  • Determines the rate of drug release from colloidal drug delivery systems
  • Used to estimate particle size of colloids
  • Important in understanding membrane transport and permeability
  • Basis for dialysis — separation of crystalloids from colloids
Important Relationship: Both Brownian motion and Diffusion are mathematically linked through the Einstein equation: D = x̄² / (2t). The mean square displacement in Brownian motion directly gives the diffusion coefficient.
Always write the mathematical equations for both properties — Einstein's displacement equation for Brownian motion and Fick's law for diffusion. Mentioning Einstein-Stokes equation adds extra value.
Q.1(c) Describe the Kawakita and Heckel equations with respect to powder compression.[05] ▼

Kawakita Equation

The Kawakita equation is used to study the compressibility of powders. It describes the relationship between the degree of volume reduction and the applied compression pressure.

C = (V₀ - V) / V₀ = a·P / (1 + b·P)

Or in linearized form:

P/C = 1/(a·b) + P/a

Where:

  • C = Degree of volume reduction = (V₀ - V)/V₀
  • V₀ = Initial volume of powder bed
  • V = Volume at pressure P
  • P = Applied compression pressure
  • a = Kawakita constant → represents the maximum compressibility (fraction of total volume that can be reduced). It ranges from 0 to 1.
  • b = Kawakita constant → related to the pressure required for significant compression. A larger 'b' means less compressible at low pressures.

Graphical Determination: A plot of P/C vs P gives a straight line with slope = 1/a and intercept = 1/(ab).

Significance:

  • 'a' value indicates the ease of compression — higher 'a' means more compressible
  • Used to compare compressibility of different powders and formulations
  • Particularly useful for fine powders that undergo significant rearrangement

Heckel Equation

The Heckel equation is based on the assumption that powder compression follows first-order kinetics, analogous to a chemical rate process. It describes the densification behavior during compaction.

ln[1/(1 - D)] = K·P + A

Where:

  • D = Relative density of the compact at pressure P = ρₐ/ρₜ
  • ρₐ = Apparent density of compact, ρₜ = True density of powder
  • P = Applied pressure
  • K = Heckel constant (slope) → related to the yield pressure (Pᵧ) of the material: Pᵧ = 1/K
  • A = Intercept → related to the densification occurring at low pressures (die filling and particle rearrangement)

Graphical Determination: A plot of ln[1/(1-D)] vs P gives a straight line with slope = K and intercept = A.

Interpretation of Heckel Plot:

  • The plot is typically non-linear at low pressures (particle rearrangement phase) and becomes linear at higher pressures (plastic deformation phase)
  • Low Pᵧ (high K): Material deforms plastically easily (e.g., microcrystalline cellulose)
  • High Pᵧ (low K): Material is hard and brittle, deforms by fragmentation (e.g., lactose, dicalcium phosphate)
  • The intercept 'A' gives information about initial packing and rearrangement

Q.2(a) With the help of a diagram discuss the principle and working of a Searle type of cup and bob viscometer.[06] ▼

Searle Type Cup and Bob Viscometer

Principle

The Searle type viscometer works on the principle of rotational viscometry. In this instrument, the inner cylinder (bob) rotates while the outer cylinder (cup) remains stationary. The viscous drag (torque) produced by the test liquid between the bob and cup is measured, which is directly related to the viscosity of the liquid.

For a Newtonian fluid, the relationship is given by:

η = T / (4π·L·ω) × (1/Rb² - 1/Rc²)⁻¹

Where:

  • η = Absolute viscosity (poise)
  • T = Torque (dyne-cm)
  • L = Effective length of bob immersed in liquid
  • ω = Angular velocity of bob (rad/s)
  • Rb = Radius of bob, Rc = Radius of cup

Construction (Diagram)

┌─────────────────────────┐ │ SCALE / POINTER │ │ ┃ │ │ ┌────┸────┐ │ │ │ TORSION │ │ │ │ WIRE │ │ │ └────┬────┘ │ │ ┃ │ │ ┌────┴────┐ │ │ │ BOB │←── Rotates│ │ │ (Inner) │ │ │ └────┬────┘ │ │ TEST LIQUID FILLED │ │ ┌────┴────┐ │ │ │ CUP │←── Fixed │ │ │(Outer) │ │ │ └─────────┘ │ │ ════════════════════ │ │ WATER JACKET │ │ (Temperature Control) │ └─────────────────────────┘ COMPONENTS: ┌──────────┬────────────────────────────────┐ │ Component │ Description │ ├──────────┼────────────────────────────────┤ │ Bob │ Inner cylinder, rotates │ │ Cup │ Outer cylinder, stationary │ │ Torsion │ Calibrated wire/spring that │ │ Wire │ measures torque via deflection │ │ Pointer │ Indicates angular deflection │ │ Scale │ Calibrated scale for reading │ │ Motor │ Drives the bob at known speed │ │ Water │ Maintains constant temperature │ │ Jacket │ during measurement │ └──────────┴────────────────────────────────┘

Working Procedure

  1. The cup is filled with the test liquid up to a marked level, ensuring the bob is fully immersed.
  2. The temperature is maintained constant using the water jacket (since viscosity is temperature-dependent).
  3. The motor is switched on, which drives the bob to rotate at a known, constant angular velocity (ω).
  4. As the bob rotates, the viscous liquid exerts a drag force (torque) on the bob, which is transmitted through the torsion wire.
  5. The torsion wire twists proportionally to the torque, causing the pointer to deflect on the calibrated scale.
  6. The deflection (θ) is read from the scale, which is proportional to the torque: T = k·θ (where k is the torsion wire constant).
  7. Using the calibration data and the formula, the viscosity is calculated.
  8. For non-Newtonian fluids, measurements are taken at different speeds (shear rates) to construct a flow curve (shear stress vs shear rate).

Applications

  • Determination of viscosity of Newtonian and non-Newtonian pharmaceutical fluids
  • Study of flow behavior of semi-solids (creams, ointments, gels)
  • Quality control of liquid dosage forms (syrups, suspensions)
  • Characterization of rheological properties of polymer solutions
Searle vs Couette: In Searle type, the bob rotates and cup is stationary. In Couette type, the cup rotates and bob is stationary. Searle type may suffer from Taylor vortices at high speeds, whereas Couette type is more stable.
Always draw a neat labeled diagram — it carries 2-3 marks. Clearly mention the formula with all symbols. Mention the difference between Searle and Couette type as extra points.
Q.2(b) Write a note on measurement of thixotropic coefficient.[05] ▼

Thixotropy and Measurement of Thixotropic Coefficient

Thixotropy

Thixotropy is a time-dependent shear thinning property. A thixotropic material shows a decrease in viscosity with time under constant shear stress, and when the stress is removed, it recovers its viscosity gradually with time. This is a reversible, isothermal process.

Examples: Flocculated suspensions, gels (e.g., bentonite magma), certain creams and ointments, magmas.

Methods of Measuring Thixotropic Coefficient

1. Hysteresis Loop Method (Most Common)

A rotational viscometer is used to plot an upward curve (increasing shear rate from 0 to maximum) and a downward curve (decreasing shear rate from maximum to 0) on a graph of shear stress (τ) vs rate of shear (D).

Shear Stress (τ) │ ╱↑ curve │ ╱ │╱ ↓ curve │╲ │ ╲ │ ╲ └──────────────── Rate of Shear (D) Area between curves = Thixotropic Breakdown

The area of the hysteresis loop between the upward and downward curves represents the degree of thixotropic breakdown — i.e., the thixotropic coefficient.

Thixotropic Coefficient = Area of Hysteresis Loop
  • Larger area → Higher thixotropy → More structural breakdown
  • Smaller area → Lower thixotropy → More stable structure
  • Zero area → No thixotropy (Newtonian or non-thixotropic)

2. Structural Recovery Method

After applying a high shear rate to break down the structure, the shear rate is suddenly reduced to a low value, and the recovery of viscosity with time is monitored.

Thixotropic Coefficient = (ηₜ - η∞) / (η₀ - η∞)

Where ηₜ = viscosity at time t, η∞ = equilibrium viscosity, η₀ = initial viscosity.

3. Coaxial Cylinder Method

Using a cup and bob viscometer, the bob is rotated at a constant speed. The torque decreases with time as the structure breaks down. The slope of the torque vs time curve gives the thixotropic coefficient.

Th = -(dτ/dt) / τ

Factors Affecting Thixotropic Coefficient

  • Concentration of dispersed phase: Higher concentration → Higher thixotropy
  • Nature of particles: Anisotropic particles show higher thixotropy
  • Temperature: Increase in temperature generally decreases thixotropy
  • pH: Affects the zeta potential and degree of flocculation
  • Electrolyte concentration: Influences the flocculation state
The hysteresis loop method is the most frequently asked. Always draw the hysteresis loop diagram showing upward and downward curves. Mention that the area can be calculated using a planimeter or by integration.
Q.2(c) With respect to Rheology describe how aqueous hydroxy propyl methyl cellulose system would behave under stress.[05] ▼

Rheological Behavior of Aqueous HPMC System Under Stress

Hydroxypropyl Methylcellulose (HPMC) is a non-ionic cellulose ether widely used in pharmaceutical formulations as a thickening agent, binder, film-former, and matrix former in controlled release tablets.

Nature of Aqueous HPMC Solutions

Aqueous HPMC solutions exhibit pseudoplastic (shear-thinning) behavior. This means the viscosity decreases with increasing shear stress (or shear rate).

Mechanism of Pseudoplastic Behavior

In the resting state (low shear), the long, entangled polymer chains of HPMC form a three-dimensional network through physical entanglements and hydrogen bonding with water molecules. This network creates significant resistance to flow → high apparent viscosity.

When stress is applied:

  1. At low shear stress: The network begins to partially disentangle. The polymer chains start to align in the direction of flow. Viscosity shows a noticeable decrease.
  2. At moderate shear stress: Progressive alignment and disentanglement of chains occurs. The structured network is increasingly broken down. Viscosity decreases more rapidly.
  3. At high shear stress: The polymer chains become fully aligned in the direction of flow. Maximum disentanglement is achieved. The solution approaches minimum viscosity.
  4. Upon removal of stress: The chains gradually re-entangle and the original viscosity is recovered over time → showing thixotropic characteristics in concentrated solutions.
Viscosity (η) │╲ │ ╲ │ ╲ │ ╲ │ ╲ │ ╲____________ └───────────────── Shear Stress (τ) Pseudoplastic Curve (HPMC)

Mathematical Description

The flow behavior can be described by the Power Law (Ostwald-de Waele) model:

τ = K · Dⁿ (where n < 1 for pseudoplastic)

Where:

  • τ = Shear stress, D = Shear rate
  • K = Consistency index (related to viscosity)
  • n = Flow behavior index (for HPMC, n < 1, typically 0.3–0.7)

Factors Influencing the Behavior

  • Concentration: Higher HPMC concentration → More entanglement → Higher zero-shear viscosity and more pronounced pseudoplasticity
  • Molecular weight/viscosity grade: Higher MW grades (e.g., HPMC K100M) show stronger pseudoplastic behavior than lower grades (e.g., HPMC K4M)
  • Temperature: Increasing temperature reduces viscosity (thermal energy overcomes hydrogen bonds), but pseudoplastic nature persists
  • pH: HPMC is non-ionic, so pH has minimal effect on its rheological behavior (unlike carbomers)
  • Added electrolytes/solvents: Can affect hydrogen bonding and modify the flow behavior

Pharmaceutical Significance

  • Suspensions: High viscosity at rest prevents settling; shear-thinning during shaking allows easy pouring
  • Ophthalmic solutions: Pseudoplasticity allows easy instillation (low viscosity during blinking) and prolonged retention (high viscosity at rest)
  • Topical gels: Easy spreading under shear, good adherence at rest
  • Matrix tablets: Gel layer formation with pseudoplastic character controls drug release
Always mention that HPMC is non-ionic — this is important because it means pH doesn't significantly affect its rheology. Also mention the Power Law equation with n < 1 as the mathematical proof of pseudoplasticity.
Q.3(a) Briefly discuss the theory of sedimentation in settling of suspension.[06] ▼

Theory of Sedimentation in Settling of Suspension

Sedimentation is the process by which suspended particles in a liquid medium settle down under the influence of gravity. It is a critical phenomenon in pharmaceutical suspensions as it directly affects physical stability, uniformity of dose, and shelf life.

Forces Acting on a Settling Particle

When a particle is suspended in a liquid, three forces act on it:

  1. Gravitational force (Fg): Acts downward, Fg = m·g = (4/3)πr³(ρs - ρL)g
  2. Buoyant force (Fb): Acts upward, Fb = (4/3)πr³ρL·g
  3. Frictional/Drag force (Fd): Acts upward, opposing motion, Fd = 6πηrv

At terminal velocity (when particle settles at constant speed): Fg - Fb = Fd

Stokes' Law

For spherical, rigid particles settling under laminar flow conditions (Reynolds number < 0.2), the sedimentation velocity is given by Stokes' equation:

v = 2r²(ρs - ρL)g / 9η

Where:

  • v = Sedimentation velocity (cm/s)
  • r = Radius of the particle (cm)
  • ρs = Density of solid particle (g/cm³)
  • ρL = Density of liquid medium (g/cm³)
  • g = Acceleration due to gravity (981 cm/s²)
  • η = Viscosity of the medium (poise)

Assumptions of Stokes' Law

  • Particles are spherical, rigid, and smooth
  • Settling occurs under laminar flow conditions (Re < 0.2)
  • Particles settle independently (dilute suspension, no interference)
  • Medium is homogeneous and infinite in extent
  • No slip occurs between particle and fluid
  • No Brownian motion (applicable for particles > 0.5 μm)

Factors Affecting Sedimentation Rate

FactorEffect
Particle size (r²)Sedimentation rate ∝ r² — doubling size increases rate 4 times
Density difference (ρs - ρL)Greater difference → Faster settling
Viscosity (η)Higher viscosity → Slower settling (inversely proportional)
Particle shapeNon-spherical particles settle slower than predicted
ConcentrationHigh concentration → hindered settling (slower than Stokes)
TemperatureHigher T → lower η → faster settling
FlocculationFlocs settle faster initially but form loose sediment

Hindered Settling

In concentrated suspensions, particles do not settle independently. The sedimentation rate is reduced due to:

  • Upward displacement of medium by settling particles
  • Particle-particle interactions
  • Modified drag forces

The corrected equation includes a void fraction term (ε):

v_h = v × ε² · f(ε)

Pharmaceutical Approaches to Reduce Sedimentation

  • Reduce particle size (since v ∝ r², most effective factor)
  • Increase viscosity of the medium using viscosity enhancers
  • Controlled flocculation — form loose flocs that settle but redispersible
  • Reduce density difference between particle and medium
Most Important Factor: Particle radius (r²) is the most influential factor in Stokes' equation. Reducing particle size is the most effective strategy to slow sedimentation. This is why micronization is widely used in suspension formulation.
Always write Stokes' equation with all symbols explained. Mention all assumptions — examiners frequently ask about assumptions. Also discuss hindered settling as it shows deeper understanding.
Q.3(b) Give a brief account on theories of emulsification.[05] ▼

Theories of Emulsification

Emulsification is the process of dispersing one immiscible liquid in another in the form of droplets, with the help of an emulsifying agent. Several theories have been proposed to explain the mechanism of emulsification:

1. Surface Tension Theory (Reduced Interfacial Tension Theory)

This is the most fundamental theory. When two immiscible liquids are brought together, a high interfacial tension exists at their boundary, which resists the creation of new interface (i.e., breakup of droplets).

Emulsifying agents (surfactants) are adsorbed at the oil-water interface, lowering the interfacial tension. The reduced interfacial tension means less energy is required to break large droplets into smaller ones during emulsification.

ΔG = γ × ΔA

Where ΔG = free energy change, γ = interfacial tension, ΔA = increase in interfacial area. Lower γ → Lower ΔG → Easier emulsification.

Limitation: Reduction of interfacial tension alone cannot explain the stability of emulsion, as it only reduces the thermodynamic driving force for coalescence but does not eliminate it.

2. Interfacial Film Theory (Adsorbed Film Theory)

Proposed by Harkins, this theory states that the emulsifying agent forms a thin, coherent film around each dispersed droplet at the interface. This film acts as a physical barrier preventing droplets from coming together and coalescing.

The nature of the film determines the type of emulsion:

  • Monomolecular film (surfactants): Stable if the film is strong and coherent
  • Multi-molecular film (hydrophilic colloids like acacia, gelatin): Form thick, viscoelastic films that provide mechanical stability
  • Solid particle film (solid emulsifiers like bentonite, veegum): Particles get adsorbed at the interface forming a rigid shell around droplets (Pickering emulsions)

Limitation: Does not explain why certain emulsifying agents preferentially stabilize O/W or W/O emulsions.

3. Oriented Wedge Theory (Bancroft's Rule)

Proposed by Bancroft and Schulman, this theory explains the type of emulsion formed based on the geometry of the emulsifying agent molecule at the interface.

The emulsifying agent molecule has a polar (hydrophilic) head and a non-polar (lipophilic) tail. The molecule is visualized as a wedge:

  • If the hydrophilic portion is larger (wider base of wedge faces water) → Stabilizes O/W emulsion
  • If the lipophilic portion is larger (wider base of wedge faces oil) → Stabilizes W/O emulsion

Bancroft's Rule: "The phase in which the emulsifying agent is more soluble becomes the continuous phase."

Limitation: Does not apply to ionic surfactants and solid particle emulsifiers. The wedge shape is an oversimplification.

4. Electrical Double Layer Theory

Applicable to O/W emulsions stabilized by ionic surfactants. The charged heads of the surfactant molecules create an electrical double layer around each droplet.

  • When droplets approach each other, the overlapping double layers create electrostatic repulsion
  • This repulsive force prevents coalescence
  • The zeta potential (typically > ±30 mV) indicates good electrostatic stability

Limitation: Applicable only to O/W emulsions with ionic emulsifiers. Addition of electrolytes can collapse the double layer and destabilize the emulsion.

For a 5-mark question, cover all 4 theories with their limitations. Mention Bancroft's Rule clearly as it's frequently tested. Use the limitation of each theory to show that no single theory explains everything — the combined effect provides stability.
Q.3(c) Explain identification test for emulsion.[05] ▼

Identification Tests for Emulsion

Identification tests are used to determine whether a given emulsion is O/W (Oil-in-Water) or W/O (Water-in-Oil) type. The following tests are commonly employed:

1. Dilution Test

Principle: An emulsion can be diluted only by its continuous phase.

  • O/W emulsion: Can be diluted with water (miscible with continuous phase) → emulsion remains stable
  • W/O emulsion: Cannot be diluted with water (immiscible with continuous oil phase) → water separates out
  • Conversely, W/O emulsion can be diluted with oil

Procedure: Add a few drops of the emulsion to a beaker of water. If it mixes freely → O/W type. If it remains as separate globules → W/O type.

2. Dye Solubility Test

Principle: A water-soluble dye will color only the continuous phase if it is O/W, while an oil-soluble dye will color the continuous phase if it is W/O.

  • Water-soluble dye (e.g., Amaranth, Methylene Blue): If the emulsion appears uniformly colored → O/W type. If only dispersed globules are colored → W/O type.
  • Oil-soluble dye (e.g., Scarlet Red, Sudan III): If uniformly colored → W/O type. If only globules colored → O/W type.

3. Conductivity Test

Principle: Water is a good conductor of electricity (especially when containing ions), whereas oil is a poor conductor.

  • O/W emulsion: Shows high electrical conductivity (water is continuous phase)
  • W/O emulsion: Shows low electrical conductivity (oil is continuous phase)

Procedure: Dip two electrodes connected to a conductivity meter (or a simple circuit with a bulb) into the emulsion. Bright bulb → O/W. Dim/no light → W/O.

4. Fluorescence Test

Principle: Many oils fluoresce under UV light, while water does not.

  • O/W emulsion: Under UV light, only scattered fluorescent spots (oil globules) are seen against a dark background
  • W/O emulsion: The entire field fluoresces uniformly (oil is continuous)

5. Filter Paper Test (Wetability Test)

Principle: Filter paper is wetted by water but not by oil.

  • O/W emulsion: A drop placed on filter paper spreads rapidly (water wets the paper)
  • W/O emulsion: A drop placed on filter paper does not spread (oil does not wet paper)

6. Cobalt Chloride Test

Principle: Cobalt chloride paper is blue when dry and turns pink when wetted by water.

  • Place a drop of emulsion on cobalt chloride paper
  • If the paper turns pink → O/W emulsion (water in continuous phase wets the paper)
  • If the paper remains blue → W/O emulsion

Q.4(a) Describe suitable method for particle volume measurement.[06] ▼

Methods for Particle Volume Measurement

Particle volume measurement is essential for determining density, porosity, and other derived properties of powders. Different methods measure different types of volume (true volume, bulk volume, or enveloped volume). The important methods are:

1. Liquid Displacement Method (True Volume)

Principle: The volume of a powder sample is determined by measuring the volume of a liquid displaced when the powder is immersed in it.

Procedure using Pycnometer:

  1. Weigh an empty, dry pycnometer → W₁
  2. Fill with liquid (of known density) and weigh → W₂
  3. Empty the pycnometer, add a known weight of powder sample → W₃
  4. Add liquid to cover the powder, remove air bubbles (by vacuum or boiling), fill to mark → W₄
Volume of powder = Vₚ = [(W₂ - W₁) - (W₄ - W₃)] / ρL

Where ρL = Density of the displacement liquid.

Requirements for the liquid:

  • Must not dissolve the powder
  • Must not be absorbed by the powder
  • Must wet the powder completely (low surface tension)
  • Must have low viscosity for easy penetration into pores
  • Common liquids: Xylene, toluene, hexane, n-heptane (for hydrophobic powders)

2. Air Pycnometer Method (Gas Displacement Method)

Principle: Uses a gas (usually helium or nitrogen) instead of liquid to determine the true volume of powder. Since gas molecules are extremely small, they penetrate even the finest pores, providing accurate true volume.

Working:

  1. A known weight of powder is placed in a sample cell of known volume (Vc)
  2. The cell is pressurized with gas to pressure P₁
  3. The gas is then allowed to expand into a reference cell of known volume (Vr)
  4. The equilibrium pressure P₂ is measured
Vp = Vc - Vr × [(P₂ - Patm) / (P₁ - P₂)]

Advantages:

  • No liquid penetration issues — gas reaches all accessible pores
  • Non-destructive, non-wetting
  • Helium is ideal due to its small atomic size and inert nature
  • More accurate than liquid displacement for fine powders

3. Mercury Porosimetry (Enveloped Volume / Pore Volume)

Principle: Mercury is a non-wetting liquid (contact angle > 90° with most solids). It does NOT penetrate pores unless external pressure is applied. The volume of mercury displaced at atmospheric pressure gives the enveloped volume (total particle volume including internal pores).

Pore radius = -2γ cosθ / P

Where γ = surface tension of mercury, θ = contact angle, P = applied pressure.

By increasing pressure stepwise, mercury penetrates progressively smaller pores, giving a pore size distribution.

4. Mercury Displacement Method (Bulk Volume)

A simple method where powder is placed in a graduated cylinder and the volume is read directly. This gives bulk volume including interparticle voids.

Key Distinction: Liquid/Gas pycnometer → True volume ( excludes pores). Mercury porosimetry at low pressure → Enveloped volume (includes pores). Graduated cylinder → Bulk volume (includes interparticle voids). Each method serves a different purpose.
Focus on the pycnometer method with step-by-step procedure and formula — it is the most commonly asked. Also mention why air/helium pycnometer is preferred over liquid displacement (avoids wetting/penetration issues).
Q.4(b) What is specific surface? Calculate specific surface for a spherical particle.[05] ▼

Specific Surface

Specific surface is defined as the total surface area per unit mass (or per unit volume) of the powder particles. It is an important derived property that influences dissolution rate, adsorption, reactivity, and bioavailability of drugs.

It can be expressed in two ways:

  • Volume-specific surface (Sv): Surface area per unit volume (cm²/cm³ or cm⁻¹)
  • Mass-specific surface (Sw): Surface area per unit mass (cm²/g or m²/g)

Derivation for a Spherical Particle

Consider a single spherical particle of diameter d (radius r = d/2).

Step 1: Surface Area of Single Sphere

A = πd²

Step 2: Volume of Single Sphere

V = (πd³)/6

Step 3: Volume-Specific Surface (Sv)

Sv = A/V = πd² / (πd³/6) = 6/d (units: cm⁻¹)

Step 4: Mass of Single Sphere

m = ρ × V = ρ × (πd³/6)

Where ρ = true density of the material (g/cm³)

Step 5: Mass-Specific Surface (Sw)

Sw = A/m = πd² / [ρ × (πd³/6)] = 6/(ρd) (units: cm²/g)

For N particles of uniform size:

Sv = 6/d (same, as specific surface is an intensive property) Sw = 6/(ρd)

Numerical Illustration

If a spherical particle has d = 10 μm = 10 × 10⁻⁴ cm and ρ = 2.5 g/cm³:

1 Sv = 6/d = 6/(10 × 10⁻⁴) = 6000 cm⁻¹
2 Sw = 6/(ρd) = 6/(2.5 × 10 × 10⁻⁴) = 2400 cm²/g = 0.24 m²/g
Key Insight: Specific surface is inversely proportional to particle diameter. Smaller particles have enormously larger specific surface. For example, reducing particle size from 10 μm to 1 μm increases Sv by 10 times. This is why micronization dramatically improves dissolution.

For Non-Spherical Particles

The equations are modified using the surface-volume shape coefficient (α):

Sv = α / dsv

For a sphere, α = 6. For other shapes, α differs (e.g., cube: α = 6, cylinder with L = d: α = 6.5).

Always derive step-by-step from basic formulas (A = πd², V = πd³/6). Show cancellation clearly. Mention both Sv and Sw forms. A small numerical illustration adds value.
Sv = 6/d (cm⁻¹), Sw = 6/(ρd) (cm²/g) for spheres. Smaller d → Larger specific surface → Faster dissolution. This is the scientific basis of micronization.
Q.4(c) Discuss the derived properties of powder.[05] ▼

Derived Properties of Powder

Derived properties are those that are calculated or derived from the fundamental properties (particle size, shape, density) of powders. They are important in pharmaceutical processing, formulation, and quality control.

1. Porosity (ε)

The fraction of the total bulk volume that is occupied by voids (air spaces) between and within particles.

ε = (Vb - Vp) / Vb = 1 - (Db/Dt)

Where Vb = Bulk volume, Vp = True volume of particles, Db = Bulk density, Dt = True density.

  • Always expressed as a fraction (0 to 1) or percentage (0% to 100%)
  • Typical range for pharmaceutical powders: 30–60%
  • Affects compressibility, flow, and dissolution

2. Packing Density (Dp)

The ratio of the true volume of particles to the total bulk volume.

Dp = Vp / Vb = Db / Dt = 1 - ε

Indicates how efficiently particles fill the available space.

3. Bulk Density (Db)

The mass of powder per unit bulk volume (including interparticle voids).

Db = W / Vb

Two types:

  • Loose bulk density (poured density): Determined by gently pouring powder into a measuring cylinder
  • Tapped bulk density: Determined after tapping the cylinder a fixed number of times

4. Tapped Density and Compressibility Index

Carr's Compressibility Index (CI) = [(Dtapped - Dloose) / Dtapped] × 100
Hausner Ratio (HR) = Dtapped / Dloose

5. Angle of Repose (θ)

The maximum angle between the horizontal surface and the slope of a conical heap of powder. It indicates flowability.

tan θ = h / r

Where h = height of cone, r = radius of base.

  • θ < 25° → Excellent flow
  • θ = 25°–30° → Good flow
  • θ = 30°–40° → Passable flow
  • θ > 40° → Poor flow

6. Specific Surface

As discussed, the surface area per unit mass (Sw) or unit volume (Sv). Important for dissolution and adsorption.

7. Permeability

The ease with which a fluid (usually air) passes through a bed of powder. Related to porosity and particle size. Measured using a permeameter.

Q.5(a) What is meant by controlled flocculation? Discuss the various means by which controlled flocculation can be achieved?[06] ▼

Controlled Flocculation

Definition

Controlled flocculation is the process of deliberately converting a deflocculated (peptized) suspension into a loosely aggregated (flocculated) suspension under controlled conditions. In this state, the particles form loose, chain-like aggregates called flocs that are held together by weak van der Waals forces and bridging mechanisms.

Why Controlled Flocculation is Needed

A deflocculated suspension has individual particles that settle independently, forming a dense, hard cake at the bottom that is very difficult to redisperse (caking). A flocculated suspension, however, settles as a loose, porous network that is easily redispersed upon shaking.

PropertyDeflocculated SuspensionFlocculated Suspension
Sedimentation rateSlow (small particles)Faster (larger flocs)
Sediment natureDense, hard cakeLoose, porous, easily redispersible
Suspension clarityClear supernatantCloudy supernatant
Flow through orificeEasierDifficult (high viscosity)

Various Means of Achieving Controlled Flocculation

1. Addition of Electrolytes

Adding a small, controlled amount of electrolyte compresses the electrical double layer around charged particles, reducing the zeta potential to a range where attractive forces dominate (typically 20–30 mV).

  • For positively charged particles: Add anions (Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻, citrate)
  • For negatively charged particles: Add cations (Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Al³⁺)
  • Schulze-Hardy rule: The flocculating power increases rapidly with valency of the counter-ion (Al³⁺ >> Ca²⁺ > Na⁺)

Important: The amount must be carefully controlled — too little has no effect, too much causes charge reversal and re-stabilization.

2. Addition of Polymers (Bridging Flocculation)

Long-chain polymers (ionic or non-ionic) adsorb onto particle surfaces and form bridges between adjacent particles, creating flocs.

  • Non-ionic polymers: e.g., Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), Polyethylene glycol (PEG)
  • Ionic polymers: e.g., Sodium alginate, Carbomer, CMC

Mechanism: Polymer chains adsorb at multiple points on different particles, linking them together. The polymer concentration must be optimal — excess polymer can cause steric stabilization instead.

3. Addition of Surfactants

Ionic surfactants can alter the surface charge of particles, while non-ionic surfactants can promote bridging. Surfactants also reduce interfacial tension and improve wetting.

  • Anionic surfactants: e.g., Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS)
  • Non-ionic surfactants: e.g., Polysorbate 80 (Tween 80)

4. pH Adjustment

Changing the pH of the medium alters the degree of ionization of particle surface groups and hence the zeta potential.

  • At or near the isoelectric point (IEP), the zeta potential approaches zero, and maximum flocculation occurs
  • Example: For bismuth subnitrate suspension, adjusting pH towards acidic range promotes flocculation

5. Combination Approach

In practice, a combination of the above methods is used. For example:

  • Adding a protective colloid (e.g., gelatin) + controlled electrolyte + surfactant
  • This provides both flocculation and steric protection against overdosing
The Flocculation Window: The goal is to reduce zeta potential to approximately ±20 to ±30 mV. Below ±20 mV, flocs become too large and settle rapidly. Above ±30 mV, the system remains deflocculated. This optimal range is called the "flocculation window."
This is a frequently asked 6-mark question. Cover all methods with mechanism and examples. The comparison table between deflocculated and flocculated suspension is essential. Mention Schulze-Hardy rule and the concept of flocculation window for extra marks.
Q.5(b) Explain accelerated stability testing in expiration dating of pharmaceutical dosage forms.[05] ▼

Accelerated Stability Testing in Expiration Dating

Introduction

Accelerated stability testing involves subjecting the pharmaceutical product to stress conditions (elevated temperature, humidity, light) that are more severe than normal storage conditions. The purpose is to predict the shelf life (expiration date) rapidly, without waiting for real-time data which may take 2-3 years.

Principle

The testing is based on the Arrhenius equation, which establishes that the rate of chemical degradation increases exponentially with temperature:

k = A · e^(-Ea/RT)

In logarithmic form:

log k = log A - Ea/(2.303·R·T)

Where:

  • k = Rate constant of degradation reaction
  • A = Frequency factor (Arrhenius constant)
  • Ea = Activation energy (cal/mol or J/mol)
  • R = Gas constant (1.987 cal/mol·K)
  • T = Absolute temperature (K)

Procedure for Expiration Dating

  1. Sample Preparation: The product is packaged in its final container-closure system.
  2. Storage Conditions: Samples are stored at multiple elevated temperatures, typically:
    • 40°C ± 2°C / 75% RH ± 5%
    • 50°C ± 2°C / appropriate RH
    • 60°C ± 2°C (for some products)
  3. Sampling: Samples are withdrawn at regular time intervals (0, 1, 2, 3 months, etc.).
  4. Analysis: Each sample is assayed for the active ingredient and degradation products. The concentration-time data at each temperature is used to determine the rate constant (k) at that temperature.
  5. Arrhenius Plot: A plot of log k vs 1/T (in K⁻¹) is constructed. This gives a straight line with slope = -Ea/(2.303R).
  6. Extrapolation: The line is extrapolated to room temperature (25°C or 30°C) to obtain k₂₅.
  7. Shelf Life Calculation: Using k₂₅ and the acceptable limit (usually 90% of label claim), the shelf life is calculated using the appropriate kinetic equation.
log k │ ● (60°C) │ ● (50°C) │ ● (40°C) │ ╲ │ ╲── Extrapolation ──→ ● (25°C) = k₂₅ │ ╲ └──────────────────── 1/T (K⁻¹) Slope = -Ea / (2.303R)

Important ICH Guidelines

  • ICH Q1A(R2): Stability Testing of New Drug Substances and Products
  • Long-term conditions: 25°C ± 2°C / 60% RH ± 5%
  • Intermediate conditions: 30°C ± 2°C / 65% RH ± 5%
  • Accelerated conditions: 40°C ± 2°C / 75% RH ± 5% (for 6 months)
  • Significant change: 5% potency loss, exceeding degradation limits, failure of specifications

Limitations

  • Arrhenius equation assumes a single degradation mechanism — if mechanism changes with temperature, prediction is invalid
  • Physical changes (phase separation, crystal growth) may not follow Arrhenius kinetics
  • Does not account for photodegradation (separate photostability testing needed per ICH Q1B)
  • Extrapolation over too wide a temperature range reduces accuracy
Always draw the Arrhenius plot (log k vs 1/T) with labeled axes. Mention ICH conditions specifically — examiners look for regulatory knowledge. State the limitation about mechanism change with temperature.
Q.5(c) Discuss any one method for determining true density and bulk density.[05] ▼

Determination of True Density — Liquid Displacement (Pycnometer) Method

Principle

True density is the mass per unit true volume of the material, excluding all pores and void spaces. It is determined by measuring the volume of liquid displaced by a known mass of powder using a pycnometer.

Dt = W / (Vpyc - Vliquid displaced)

Apparatus

  • Pycnometer (specific gravity bottle) — typically 10 ml or 25 ml capacity
  • Analytical balance
  • Displacement liquid (xylene, toluene, or n-heptane)
  • Vacuum desiccator (for deaeration)
  • Thermostated water bath

Procedure

  1. Weigh empty pycnometer: Clean, dry pycnometer is weighed → W₁
  2. Weigh pycnometer with liquid: Fill with displacement liquid up to the mark, maintain constant temperature, wipe excess liquid, weigh → W₂
  3. Weigh pycnometer with powder: Empty the pycnometer, dry it, add a known weight of powder sample (about 5 g), weigh → W₃
  4. Weigh pycnometer with powder + liquid: Add displacement liquid to partially fill, place in vacuum desiccator to remove entrapped air bubbles, then fill to the mark, weigh → W₄

Calculations

1 Weight of liquid filling pycnometer = W₂ - W₁
2 Volume of pycnometer = (W₂ - W₁) / ρL
3 Weight of powder = W₃ - W₁
4 Weight of liquid added with powder = W₄ - W₃
5 Volume of liquid displaced by powder = [(W₂ - W₁) - (W₄ - W₃)] / ρL
6 True Density, Dt = (W₃ - W₁) / {[(W₂ - W₁) - (W₄ - W₃)] / ρL}

Where ρL = density of the displacement liquid at the experimental temperature.

Determination of Bulk Density — Graduated Cylinder Method

Principle

Bulk density is the mass of powder per unit bulk volume, which includes the volume of particles plus the volume of interparticle void spaces.

Db = W / Vb

Procedure for Loose Bulk Density

  1. Weigh a clean, dry graduated cylinder → W₁
  2. Gently pour the powder into the cylinder from a constant height (about 2 cm) until a known volume (e.g., 50 ml) is obtained
  3. Level the surface without compacting → Read the volume Vb
  4. Weigh the cylinder with powder → W₂
  5. Loose Bulk Density = (W₂ - W₁) / Vb

Procedure for Tapped Bulk Density

  1. After determining loose bulk density, tap the cylinder mechanically or manually (typically 500–1250 taps) until the powder volume no longer changes
  2. Read the final tapped volume Vt
  3. Tapped Bulk Density = (W₂ - W₁) / Vt

The tapped density is always greater than or equal to the loose bulk density, as tapping reduces interparticle voids.

Relationship: True Density > Tapped Bulk Density ≥ Loose Bulk Density. This is because: True density excludes all voids, tapped bulk density includes interparticle voids (reduced by tapping), and loose bulk density includes maximum interparticle voids.
For each method, clearly list the steps with the weights (W₁, W₂, etc.) and write the final formula separately. The step-by-step calculation approach is what GTU examiners look for.
Q.6(a) Explain zero order and pseudo zero order reaction with examples and suitable applications.[06] ▼

Zero Order Reaction

Definition

A zero order reaction is one in which the rate of reaction is independent of the concentration of the reactant. The rate remains constant throughout the reaction.

Rate = -dC/dt = k₀

Where k₀ = zero order rate constant (concentration/time, e.g., mg/ml/min)

Integrated Equation

C = C₀ - k₀t

Where C₀ = initial concentration, C = concentration at time t.

Graphical Representation

  • Plot of C vs t → Straight line with slope = -k₀ and intercept = C₀
  • Plot of log C vs t → Curved line (NOT a straight line)

Half-Life

t½ = C₀ / (2k₀)

Half-life depends on initial concentration (unlike first order).

Characteristics

  • Rate is constant regardless of concentration
  • Units of k₀ = concentration/time
  • t½ is proportional to C₀
  • Complete degradation occurs in finite time (t = C₀/k₀)

Examples

  • Suspensions: Drug degradation in suspensions follows zero order because the dissolved drug (in solution) degrades, but the solid drug continuously dissolves to maintain constant concentration (as long as excess solid is present)
  • Transdermal patches: Drug release from reservoir-type transdermal patches at constant rate
  • Controlled release matrix tablets: Some erosion-controlled systems release drug at zero order rate
  • Infusion pumps: Constant rate IV infusion (zero order input)

Pseudo Zero Order Reaction

Definition

A pseudo zero order reaction is a reaction that is not truly zero order in its fundamental mechanism, but appears to follow zero order kinetics under specific experimental conditions due to the presence of a reservoir or buffer that maintains a constant effective concentration.

Mechanism

The actual reaction may be first order (or other order), but because of a condition that keeps the reactant concentration constant, the observed rate becomes constant:

  • The reaction consumes the reactant
  • The reservoir continuously replenishes the reactant
  • Net effect: Reactant concentration remains constant → Rate is constant → Appears zero order

Classic Example: Drug Degradation in Suspension

This is the most important pharmaceutical example:

  1. The drug exists as solid particles + dissolved drug in solution
  2. The dissolved drug degrades by a first order mechanism in solution
  3. As dissolved drug degrades, more drug dissolves from the solid particles to maintain saturation solubility (Cs)
  4. Since the concentration in solution remains constant at Cs (as long as solid drug is present), the degradation rate is constant
Rate = -dC/dt = k₁ · Cs = k₀ (constant)

This is pseudo zero order because: it is actually first order (k₁), but the concentration is held constant (Cs) by the solid reservoir.

Conditions for Pseudo Zero Order Behavior

  • Excess solid drug must be present (to maintain saturation)
  • The dissolution rate must be faster than the degradation rate
  • Once all solid dissolves, the reaction reverts to its true order (first order)

Applications

  • Suspension formulation: Pseudo zero order degradation means predictable shelf life
  • Controlled drug delivery systems: Reservoir systems, osmotic pumps designed for zero order release
  • Surface erosion-controlled devices: Constant surface area erosion gives pseudo zero order release
ParameterZero OrderPseudo Zero Order
True mechanismGenuinely zero orderActually first order (or other)
Rate = k₀k₁ × Cs (appears constant)
Requires reservoir?NoYes (e.g., excess solid)
Behavior changeAlways zero orderReverts to true order when reservoir exhausted
The suspension example is the key to answering this question well. Clearly explain WHY it appears zero order (saturation solubility maintained by excess solid). Always present the comparison between true zero order and pseudo zero order.
Zero order: Rate = k₀, independent of [C]. Pseudo zero order: Rate = k₁ × Cs (constant [C] maintained by reservoir). Suspension is the classic example — solid dissolves to replace degraded drug, keeping [C] = Cs.
Q.6(b) A solution of a drug contained 500 units/ml when prepared. It was analyzed after 40 days and was found to contain 300 units/ml. Assuming it a first order reaction, find out half life and shelf life of the solution.[05] ▼

Numerical: First Order Kinetics — Half Life and Shelf Life

Given Data

Given Initial concentration, C₀ = 500 units/ml
Given Concentration after 40 days, C = 300 units/ml
Given Time, t = 40 days
Given Reaction order = First order

Step 1: Calculate the Rate Constant (k)

First order integrated equation:

k = 2.303/t × log(C₀/C)
1 k = 2.303/40 × log(500/300)
2 k = 0.05758 × log(1.6667)
3 k = 0.05758 × 0.22185
4 k = 0.01277 day⁻¹

Step 2: Calculate Half Life (t½)

For first order reaction:

t½ = 0.693 / k
1 t½ = 0.693 / 0.01277
2 t½ = 54.27 days

Step 3: Calculate Shelf Life (t₉₀)

Shelf life is the time at which the drug retains 90% of its original potency:

t₉₀ = 0.1054 / k (since C = 0.9C₀)

Derivation: k = 2.303/t₉₀ × log(C₀/0.9C₀) = 2.303/t₉₀ × log(1.111) = 2.303/t₉₀ × 0.04576 = 0.1054/t₉₀

1 t₉₀ = 0.1054 / 0.01277
2 t₉₀ = 8.25 days

Final Answers

Rate constant (k) = 0.01277 day⁻¹
Half life (t½) = 54.27 days
Shelf life (t₉₀) = 8.25 days

Verification

Check: Using t₉₀ = 8.25 days and C₀ = 500 units/ml:

Check C at t₉₀ = C₀ × e^(-kt) = 500 × e^(-0.01277 × 8.25) = 500 × e^(-0.1054) = 500 × 0.8999 ≈ 450 units/ml = 90% of 500 ✓
In GTU numericals, always: (1) Write given data first, (2) Write the formula before substituting, (3) Show all intermediate steps, (4) Write final answer with proper units, (5) Do a verification step if time permits. The shelf life formula t₉₀ = 0.1054/k must be derived or stated — don't use it without explanation.
Q.6(c) Discuss the various means of stabilization of product which is sensitive to oxidation.[05] ▼

Stabilization of Oxidation-Sensitive Pharmaceutical Products

Oxidation is one of the most common degradation pathways for pharmaceutical products. Drugs containing phenolic groups, unsaturated bonds, aldehydes, thiols, and amines are particularly susceptible. The following strategies are employed to stabilize such products:

1. Use of Antioxidants

Antioxidants are substances that are preferentially oxidized, thereby protecting the drug molecule. They act by donating electrons/hydrogen atoms to free radicals, breaking the chain reaction of oxidation.

Types of Antioxidants:

  • Primary antioxidants (Chain-breaking):
    • Sodium metabisulfite (Na₂S₂O₅)
    • Sodium bisulfite (NaHSO₃)
    • Sodium sulfite (Na₂SO₃)
    • Ascorbic acid (Vitamin C)
    • α-Tocopherol (Vitamin E)
    • Butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT)
    • Butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA)
  • Synergists: Substances that enhance the activity of antioxidants, e.g., citric acid, phosphoric acid, ascorbyl palmitate

2. Use of Chelating Agents (Sequestrants)

Trace amounts of metal ions (Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, Cu²⁺, Mn²⁺) act as powerful catalysts for oxidation (by generating free radicals via Fenton reaction). Chelating agents bind these metal ions, rendering them inactive.

  • Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and its salts (Disodium EDTA, Calcium disodium EDTA) — most widely used
  • Citric acid — also acts as a pH adjuster and synergist
  • Tartaric acid
  • Diphosphates (e.g., sodium hexametaphosphate)
Fenton Reaction: Fe²⁺ + H₂O₂ → Fe³⁺ + •OH + OH⁻ (highly reactive hydroxyl radical)

3. Replacement of Oxygen with Inert Gas

Removing oxygen from the container headspace eliminates the oxidizing agent.

  • Nitrogen (N₂) — most commonly used, cost-effective
  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂) — used for some products, but can affect pH
  • Argon — expensive, used for highly sensitive biologics

Process: The container is flushed with inert gas before and/or after filling, and the headspace is purged. Called nitrogen blanketing.

4. Storage in Airtight Containers

Preventing atmospheric oxygen from reaching the product:

  • Use of amber glass bottles with tight seals (prevents both O₂ and light entry)
  • Sealed ampoules for parenteral products
  • Blister packs with aluminum foil backing
  • Use of oxygen-impermeable packaging materials

5. Protection from Light

Light (especially UV) can initiate free radical formation, leading to photo-oxidation.

  • Amber-colored glass containers
  • Opaque or light-resistant secondary packaging
  • Coating tablets with light-resistant film
  • Storage in dark conditions

6. pH Control

The rate of oxidation is often pH-dependent. Adjusting pH to a range where oxidation is minimal:

  • Many oxidations are catalyzed by OH⁻ ions (base-catalyzed)
  • Lowering pH often reduces oxidation rate
  • Use of suitable buffer systems to maintain optimal pH

7. Storage at Low Temperature

Lowering storage temperature reduces the rate of oxidation (per Arrhenius equation). Products may be stored at 2–8°C (refrigeration) or even frozen.

8. Reduction of Water Content

Water can participate in oxidation reactions. Reducing water content (lyophilization, use of anhydrous vehicles) can improve stability.

Combination Approach: In practice, multiple strategies are used simultaneously. For example: EDTA (chelator) + Sodium metabisulfite (antioxidant) + Nitrogen flushing + Amber container + Refrigeration → Maximum protection against oxidation.
Cover at least 6 methods. The mechanism of chelating agents (preventing Fenton reaction) and the distinction between primary antioxidants and synergists are important points. Always mention EDTA as the most common chelating agent.
Q.7(a) A plastic material was found to have a yield value of 5200 dyne/cm². At shearing stresses above the yield value, shearing stress and rate of shear were linearly proportional. If rate of shear is 150 sec⁻¹ when shearing stress was 8000 dyne/cm², calculate the mobility of the system.[05] ▼

Numerical: Mobility of a Bingham Plastic System

Given Data

Given Yield value (f) = 5200 dyne/cm²
Given Shearing stress (τ) = 8000 dyne/cm²
Given Rate of shear (D) = 150 sec⁻¹
Given Above yield value, τ and D are linearly proportional → Bingham Plastic behavior

Concept

The system follows the Bingham plastic model:

τ = f + ηD

Where:

  • τ = Total shearing stress (dyne/cm²)
  • f = Yield value (dyne/cm²)
  • η = Plastic viscosity (poise)
  • D = Rate of shear (sec⁻¹)

Mobility is defined as the reciprocal of plastic viscosity:

Mobility (φ) = 1/η

Units: cm²/dyne·sec (or rheopoise⁻¹)

Step 1: Calculate Plastic Viscosity (η)

τ = f + ηD
1 8000 = 5200 + η × 150
2 8000 - 5200 = η × 150
3 2800 = η × 150
4 η = 2800 / 150
5 η = 18.667 poise

Step 2: Calculate Mobility (φ)

φ = 1/η
1 φ = 1 / 18.667
2 φ = 0.05357 cm²/dyne·sec

Final Answer

Plastic Viscosity (η) = 18.667 poise
Mobility (φ) = 0.0536 cm²/dyne·sec

Verification

Check τ = f + D/φ = 5200 + 150/0.05357 = 5200 + 2800.04 ≈ 8000 dyne/cm² ✓
Note Alternatively: τ = f + D × η = 5200 + 150 × 18.667 = 5200 + 2800.05 ≈ 8000 dyne/cm² ✓

Interpretation

  • The material requires a minimum stress of 5200 dyne/cm² before it begins to flow (yield value)
  • Once flowing, it behaves like a Newtonian fluid with plastic viscosity of 18.667 poise
  • Mobility of 0.0536 cm²/dyne·sec indicates moderate fluidity once the yield value is exceeded
